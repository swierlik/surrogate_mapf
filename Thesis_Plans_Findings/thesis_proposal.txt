Accelerating Lifelong Mapf Optimization Via Surrogate-Assisted Evolution Strategies
Description of the assignment: 
Lifelong Multi-Agent Path Finding (LMAPF) has become the operational backbone of modern automated 
logistics, driving the fleets of thousands of robots that power fulfillment centers like those of Amazon and Ocado. 
Unlike traditional pathfinding, where agents stop after reaching a destination, LMAPF is a continuous process where 
agents receive new goals immediately upon completion. The current state-of-the-art approach to managing this 
complexity involves Guidance Graph Optimization, where an evolutionary algorithm, typically CMA-ES (Covariance 
Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy), learns a set of "flow weights" overlaying the warehouse map. These learned 
weights effectively act as invisible traffic laws, guiding decentralized agents into high-throughput "highways" and 
preventing congestion before it forms.
However, a critical bottleneck currently limits the scalability of this approach: the extreme computational 
cost of evaluation. To determine the quality of a single set of flow weights, the optimizer must run a full multi-agent 
simulation, often requiring hundreds of timesteps to reach a stable throughput metric. Since standard evolutionary 
strategies require thousands of such evaluations to converge, a single optimization run can take days of compute 
time. This inefficiency renders the method impractical for dynamic environments where warehouse layouts or goal 
distributions shift frequently - a limitation explicitly highlighted as a key area for future work in recent 2025 robotics 
literature.
The objective of this bachelor thesis is to overcome this barrier by implementing and evaluating a Surrogate Assisted Optimization framework. The core hypothesis is that we can drastically reduce the number of expensive 
simulations required by training a computationally cheap machine learning model, the "Surrogate", to approximate 
the simulator’s behavior. Instead of blindly running every candidate solution generated by the optimizer through the 
heavy simulation, the system will use the Surrogate to pre-screen candidates, predicting their likely throughput in 
milliseconds.
The methodology will center on constructing an active learning loop around the optimization process. I will 
implement a standard CMA-ES optimizer in Python, interacting with a pre-made grid-based LMAPF simulator (such 
as POGEMA or gym-mapf). The innovation lies in the integration of a regression model, initially a Random Forest for 
stability, later upgrading to a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) in PyTorch, that learns the mapping from edge weights 
to global throughput. In each generation, the optimizer will propose a population of candidates, but the surrogate 
will filter them, selecting only the most promising few for "ground truth" evaluation in the real simulator. 
These real evaluations will then be fed back into the dataset to retrain and refine the Surrogate online, 
ensuring it becomes increasingly accurate as the optimization progresses. To ensure robustness, I will also explore 
"evolution control" techniques, such as periodically running a full generation on the real simulator to prevent the 
model from drifting.
Ultimately, this project aims to demonstrate that "learning to optimize" is a viable path for scaling multi agent systems. By benchmarking the Surrogate-Assisted approach against a standard "vanilla" CMA-ES baseline, I 
expect to show a significant reduction in the wall-clock time required to reach near-optimal throughput. This would 
represent a meaningful contribution to the state-of-the-art, offering a practical solution to the computational 
inefficiency that currently holds back real-time adaptation in large-scale robot swarms.
